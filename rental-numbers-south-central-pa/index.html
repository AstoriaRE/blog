Running the numbers on a South Central PA rental: a clear example

Astoria Realty Advisors · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Investing in rentals or small multifamily in South Central Pennsylvania benefits from a disciplined, numbers-first approach. This post answers a practical question investors ask: how do I run the key rental metrics (cap rate, cash flow, cash-on-cash) and what expenses should I plan for? We’ll walk through a simple, local example and label every number clearly so you can adapt it to your own deals.

What you should be able to answer before you buy

When evaluating a rental in Lancaster County or nearby towns, you want a clear sense of what the property will produce after all costs. A common question is: what will my cap rate be, and how much cash flow can I expect month to month after financing? The cap rate compares net operating income to purchase price, while cash flow and cash-on-cash focus on actual dollars after debt service and financing costs. A careful calculation also highlights expenses that buyers often overlook, such as vacancy reserves, ongoing maintenance, capital expenditures, and holding costs. For practical context, Lancaster County market data in 2026 shows ongoing activity in rental markets, with rent levels and local cap rate trends discussed by multiple sources referenced below as you run your own numbers.

Key metrics: what they mean and how to calculate

Cap rate (unlevered return)

Cap rate = net operating income (NOI) / purchase price. NOI = gross rents minus operating expenses (excluding debt service). It gives a rough comparator across deals, independent of financing. In Pennsylvania markets, cap rates for multifamily assets vary by submarket and property type; local sources in 2026 describe ranges and context for sensibly priced deals. For our example, you’ll see NOI and cap rate worked out step by step.

Cash flow (monthly)

Cash flow is the net amount you actually receive each month after all cash expenses and debt service. It is the monthly rent minus operating expenses, taxes, insurance, repairs, vacancy, and debt service (mortgage principal and interest). Positive cash flow supports monthly liquidity and long-term sustainability.

Cash-on-cash return (CoC)

CoC return measures annual pre-tax cash flow as a percentage of your initial down payment (or equity tied to the deal). CoC = (annualized pre-tax cash flow) / (initial cash investment). This helps you compare deals with different down payments or financing structures.

Common overlooked expenses to plan for

  • Vacancy: set a realistic vacancy assumption based on local market data (e.g., a range commonly cited for South Central PA neighborhoods).
  • Maintenance and repairs: reserve for ongoing upkeep (a common planning range is a portion of gross rents).
  • Capital expenditures (Capex): set aside for major items (roof, HVAC, water heater) on a multi-year horizon.
  • Property taxes and insurance: verify current tax rates by school district and municipality; insurance varies with property type and coverage.
  • Property management: if you’re not self-managing, include management fees (often a percentage of rent).
  • Accounting and legal: keep records, and consult a CPA for tax specifics in Pennsylvania.

Worked example: a simple rental in South Central PA

We’ll use a straightforward, hypothetical 4-unit property in a Lancaster County corridor. Numbers are labeled so you can adapt them to your own property and market data. Always confirm local taxes, insurance, and lender terms with professionals in your network.

Assumptions

  1. Purchase price: $900,000
  2. Down payment (cash investment): $180,000 (20%)
  3. Loan terms: 30-year fixed at 7.0% interest (illustrative; actual rates vary)
  4. Gross monthly rent: $8,000 (across all units)
  5. Annual property taxes: verify with the local tax authority
  6. Annual homeowner’s insurance: verify with insurer
  7. Annual property management (if any): 8% of gross rents
  8. Maintenance and repairs: 6% of gross rents
  9. Vacancy rate: 5% of gross rents
  10. Capital expenditures reserve: $5,000 per year

Step 1 — calculate gross and operating numbers

  • Gross annual rents: $8,000 per month × 12 = $96,000
  • Vacancy loss (5%): $96,000 × 0.05 = $4,800
  • Effective gross income (EGI): $96,000 − $4,800 = $91,200
  • Operating expenses (excluding debt service):
    • Taxes: verify with local authority
    • Insurance: verify with insurer
    • Property management (8% of gross rents): $96,000 × 0.08 = $7,680
    • Maintenance/repairs (6% of gross rents): $96,000 × 0.06 = $5,760
    • Capex reserve: $5,000
  • Total operating expenses: taxes + insurance + $7,680 + $5,760 + $5,000
  • Net operating income (NOI): EGI − operating expenses

Step 2 — cap rate (unlevered return)

Cap rate = NOI / Purchase price. Use your verified NOI and purchase price to compute the rate for your deal.

Step 3 — debt service and monthly cash flow

Monthly mortgage payment (P&I) for a $720,000 loan at 7.0% for 30 years ≈ $4,795. With annual P&I ≈ $57,540.

Annual debt service: ≈ $57,540

Annual cash flow before taxes = NOI − debt service

Monthly cash flow ≈ NOI − debt service divided by 12

Step 4 — cash-on-cash return (CoC)

Annual pre-tax cash flow = NOI − debt service

CoC = (annual pre-tax cash flow) / (initial cash investment) • Use your verified numbers for an accurate figure.

Important takeaways from the worked example

  • Financing can compress cash flow even when demand is solid. The cap rate provides a baseline; debt service determines actual cash flow.
  • Every line item matters. Vacancy, management, maintenance, and Capex reserves shift NOI and cash flow.
  • A realistic down payment and conservative debt terms influence CoC and long-term equity buildup.

FAQ

What is a reasonable cap rate for a small multifamily in South Central PA?

Cap rates vary by submarket and asset type. In 2026, sources discuss multifamily cap rates in Lancaster-area markets in a range that often falls around 6% or higher for well-priced assets, but always verify current local data and compare to nearby markets. See sources cited at the bottom for currency and context.

How should I set my vacancy and capex reserves?

Use local market data and your property type to set realistic vacancy and reserve for Capex. A conservative approach reduces surprise costs and protects cash flow.

Should I buy with owner financing, a conventional loan, or cash?

That depends on your risk tolerance, down payment availability, and cash flow goals. A higher leverage loan will typically reduce monthly cash flow but can accelerate equity growth; a lower leverage or cash purchase can increase monthly cash flow but ties up more capital. Work with a lender to model several scenarios for your target property in Lancaster County or neighboring counties.
